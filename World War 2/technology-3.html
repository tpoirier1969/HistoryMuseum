Long Rnage Bombers and Strategic Bombing

During the 1920s air strategists such as Giulio Douhet of Italy, Hugh Trenchard of Great Britain and Billy Mitchell of the U.S. developed concepts of using air power to avoid the horrors of trench warfare experienced during World War I. They saw that air power would be decisive in the next war. A country’s industrial base, civilian population centers and transportation systems could be destroyed by bombing; thus breaking the ability and will of the enemy to a prolong war. Their concepts would provide the foundation of the Allied strategic bombing doctrine of World War II. Dramatic increases in aviation technology and aircraft design during the 1930s allowed the U.S. Army Air Corps to develop a series of robust four engine long ranger bombers. Coupled with a new computer assisted bomb sight, the U.S. would put its theories on strategic bombing to test in the skies over Europe and the Pacific during WWII. While the Allied strategic bombing campaigns played an important role in the defeat of the Axis powers, they were not as decisive as thought by the pre war theorists. The USAAF and RAF Bomber Command blasted German cities to rubble, using mainly B-17 an B-24 heavy bombers, but it was not until ground forces occupied German territory that the war in Europe was ended. In the Pacific it was not until the Army, Navy and Marine Corps captured enough territory within range of Japan that the strategic bombing of Japan could begin.
